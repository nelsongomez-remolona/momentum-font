Two-thirds of Earth's meteorites were found on one empty continent

Of 45,716 catalogued meteorites, 30,646 (67%) come from Antarctica — yet they cluster in a handful of blue-ice fields, almost all in the east. This maps 22,099 Antarctic finds that carry coordinates, looking down at the South Pole.

What the geography says

01

The rocks pool against a mountain wall

Meteorites fall evenly over the planet, but Antarctica's ice flows — carrying them toward the Transantarctic Mountains, where the ice stalls, wind sublimates the surface away, and the stones surface on exposed blue ice. Nearly every cluster on the map (Allan Hills, Elephant Moraine, Queen Alexandra Range, Lewis Cliff) sits upstream of that barrier.

02

East Antarctica gets them; the West is empty

The find cloud fills the right and upper-right of the map — Dronning Maud Land (Yamato, Asuka) and Victoria Land — while West Antarctica and the Weddell sector are bare. East Antarctica is old, high, cold, and slow-flowing: it preserves and concentrates. The West is younger, faster, and drains its ice (and any meteorites) into the sea.

03

The blank spot is where the people are

The Antarctic Peninsula — warmest, wettest, with the most research stations and tourists — has essentially no meteorites. Snow buries them, melt destroys them, and there's no blue-ice conveyor to expose them. Meteorite geography is the inverse of human geography here: the rocks are where nobody lives.

04

Two programs drew the map

The eastern arc is Japanese (Yamato, Asuka); the Transantarctic clusters are the U.S. ANSMET deep-field camps. What looks like a map of where meteorites are is partly a map of where two expedition programs chose to search — richness follows the ice, but the catalogue follows the searchers.

Data: The Meteoritical Society "Meteorite Landings" catalogue (NASA Open Data / figshare mirror). 22,099 of 30,646 Antarctic finds have usable coordinates; catalogue snapshot ends ~2013. Projection: south-polar azimuthal (colatitude 90°+lat).
